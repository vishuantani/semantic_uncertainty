Estimator notes · semantic uncertainty

Estimating Semantic Entropy

Entropy is an average of surprise. You can never sum over every outcome, so you sample — and the moment you do, the question stops being “what is the entropy” and becomes “which direction is my answer wrong in.”

Subject Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar (2023), reference implementation Pilot run_1 · 40 questions · opt-350m · 5 generations
01

Entropy is average surprise

The definition, with the minus sign that is easy to drop:

H(X) = − Σi p(xi) log p(xi)

Probabilities are at most 1, so log p is never positive. The leading minus is what makes entropy come out as a non-negative number.

The cleaner way to read it is to treat −log p(x) as the surprise of an outcome. A certain event has surprise −log 1 = 0. A coin flip has −log 0.5 ≈ 0.69. A near-impossible event that happens anyway has surprise approaching infinity. Then the formula is nothing more than an expectation:

H(X) = Σi p(xi) · [surprise of xi] = E[−log p(X)]

Average surprise. Each outcome’s surprise, weighted by how often it actually occurs. Every estimator below is an attempt to compute that average without being able to enumerate the outcomes.

Two conventions worth pinning

  • The base of the log sets the units. log₂ gives bits, so a fair coin is exactly 1. Natural log gives nats, so a fair coin is 0.69. This codebase is nats throughout — torch.log and logsumexp.
  • Entropy belongs to a distribution, not an outcome. The paper writes SE(x) where x is the question being conditioned on, and the sum runs over meaning classes c. Two lowercase symbols playing entirely different roles.
02

When you can score your samples

Suppose you can draw from p and evaluate p(x) at whatever you drew. This is the comfortable case, and it has an exact answer. Draw x⁽¹⁾…x⁽ᴹ⁾ ∼ p and average their surprises:

Ĥ = −(1/M) Σm=1..M log p(x⁽ᵐ⁾)

This is exactly unbiased. The proof is one line — E[Ĥ] = −E[log p(X)] = H(X) — because you are averaging independent draws of the random variable −log p(X), whose mean is the entropy by definition. There is no approximation anywhere in it.

Its variance is Var(log p(X)) / M, sometimes called the varentropy. Error shrinks like 1/√M with no systematic floor underneath it.

Why the 1/M is not bookkeeping

In the exact formula the weights are p(xi), and they sum to 1. When you switch to sampling you stop writing those weights down — instead you draw from p, so frequent outcomes turn up often and rare ones seldom. Each sample now carries equal weight, and there are M of them.

The 1/M is the probability weight, relocated. Drop it and your weights sum to M instead of 1, so the answer would grow with however many samples you happened to draw.

Which means: whatever a line of code divides by declares the weighting it assumes. Hold on to that — it returns in §06.

The paper’s Equation 4 is precisely this estimator, applied to meaning classes rather than raw sequences, which is why its unbiasedness claim is legitimate as stated:

SE(x) ≈ −|C|⁻¹ Σi=1..|C| log p(Ci|x)

The catch is the phrase as stated. The guarantee assumes p(C_i|x) is known exactly. It is not.

03

When you can only sample

Now remove the ability to evaluate p. You have samples and nothing else. The obvious move is to build the empirical distribution first — p̂(x) = count(x)/M — and drop it into the formula:

Ĥplug-in = − Σx p̂(x) log p̂(x)

This underestimates entropy, for two reasons that need no algebra:

  • Outcomes you never sampled contribute nothing at all, though they carry real entropy.
  • Random sampling makes the empirical distribution look lumpier than the truth — some outcomes over-represented by luck — and lumpier means lower entropy.

The leading term is known, with K the number of distinct outcomes:

E[Ĥplug-in] ≈ H − (K−1)/(2M)

Adding that back is the Miller–Madow correction. Better estimators exist for when K is comparable to M — Grassberger, Chao–Shen, NSB — and that hard regime is exactly where five generations per question puts you.

04

When you must estimate p by sampling too

There is a third situation, and it is the one that actually applies here. You cannot evaluate p, but rather than collapse to counting you estimate p at each sampled point using further sampling, then feed that into the §02 form. Two nested levels:

out
Outer drawSample x⁽¹⁾…x⁽ᴹ⁾ ∼ p. These are the points where you need −log p.
in
Inner drawAt each x⁽ᵐ⁾, take N further samples and form an estimate p̂(x⁽ᵐ⁾) — say, how often it recurs.
Ĥ = −(1/M) Σm=1..M log p̂(x⁽ᵐ⁾)

The outer level stays unbiased. All the damage happens in the inner one.

The delta method, with a number attached

Even with p̂ perfectly unbiased for p, the −log breaks it. Write f(p) = −log p, note f″(p) = 1/p², and expand around the true value:

E[−log p̂] ≈ −log p + Var(p̂) / (2p²)

The bias is half the relative variance of the inner estimate, and it is positive — entropy comes out too high. That is Jensen’s inequality made quantitative.

Specialise to a frequency inner estimator, p̂ = k/N with k ∼ Binomial(N, p), so Var(p̂) = p(1−p)/N. The bias at a point is (1−p)/(2Np). Average over the outer draws — and the p(x) in the denominator cancels against the sampling weight:

E[Ĥ] − H ≈ (1/2N) Σx (1 − p(x)) = +(K−1)/(2N)

What the inner level costs

  • You need N ≫ 1/p(x). The bias term blows up as p → 0. LLM sequence probabilities run around e⁻³⁰, which puts the required N astronomically out of reach.
  • p̂ can come out zero, and −log 0 = ∞. With small p that is the common case, not an edge case. Every fix — Laplace smoothing, discarding, flooring — reintroduces bias of unknown sign.
05

The bracket

Put the two bias terms side by side and something useful falls out.

Leading bias by estimator
EstimatorRequiresLeading bias
Scored samplesEvaluate p0
Plug-in, samples reusedSamples only−(K−1)/2M
Nested, fresh inner drawsSamples only+(K−1)/2N

The symmetry also explains something that looks like a defect. Sample reuse in the plug-in estimator introduces a correlation between “this outcome is in the average” and “this outcome’s probability estimate” — and that correlation partly cancels the Jensen inflation. Splitting the samples to remove the correlation removes the cancellation too.

What actually helps

  • Sample splitting / cross-fitting. Disjoint sets for the outer point and its inner estimate. Kills the correlation term, not the Jensen term.
  • Leave-one-out. Estimate p̂₋ₘ from everything except sample m. Same benefit, no wasted data.
  • Explicit correction. Subtract the estimated (K̂−1)/2N, with the sign appropriate to your direction.
  • Jackknife. Recompute dropping each sample, extrapolate away the 1/N term. Works when you cannot estimate K.
  • k-nearest-neighbour (Kozachenko–Leonenko). For continuous x, read local density off the distance to the k-th nearest sample. Sidesteps zero counts entirely.
The result that closes the door

Paninski (2003) proved that for discrete distributions, no unbiased estimator of entropy exists from a finite sample — for any sample size, whatever the estimator.

So “what is the unbiased estimator?” has no answer once you leave §02. Every method is a bias–variance trade, and the honest goal is a bias whose sign and magnitude you know, not one that is absent.

06

Where this lands in the code

Semantic entropy looks like the comfortable §02 case: you can score any sequence under the LLM, so p(s|x) is available. But the estimand is not a sequence probability — it is a meaning class probability, which sums over every sequence carrying that meaning:

p(c|x) = Σs∈c p(s|x) ← Eq. 2, not evaluable

That sum cannot be evaluated, only estimated. So the implementation is §02 at the outer level and §03/§04 at the inner level, and Equation 4’s guarantee does not reach the thing it is applied to. Here is where each piece comes from:

1
Samplegenerate.py draws M generations per question. Monte Carlo from the start — nothing is enumerated.
2
Scoreget_likelihoods.py runs each generation teacher-forced with the prompt masked to -100, then multiplies the mean cross-entropy by the token count to recover the summed log p(s|x).
3
Clusterget_semantic_similarities.py groups generations into meanings using bidirectional entailment under deberta-large-mnli.
4
Aggregatecompute_confidence_measure.py sums member likelihoods per class in log space — this line is the inner estimator, and the three deviations below all live on it.

Deviation 1 · duplicates are summed with multiplicity

get_semantic_similarities.py:138 maps cluster ids back over generated_texts, not unique_generated_texts, so an identical string sampled three times contributes its probability three times. Taking expectations over the sampling:

E[p̂(Ci|x)] = M · Σs∈Ci p(s|x)² ← target was Σ p(s|x)

That is a second moment, scaled by M — a different functional, not a noisy version of the right one. It does not converge as M grows; it grows with M. Repeated sampling of one answer is evidence of confidence, and here it inflates the class’s estimated mass instead.

Collapsing duplicates first leaves only the benign coverage bias, downward and vanishing as M → ∞:

E[p̂(Ci|x)] = Σs∈Ci p(s|x)·(1 − (1−p(s|x))M) < p(Ci|x)

Deviation 2 · the divisor assumes what it should measure

Recall from §02 that the divisor declares the weighting. The two entropy functions disagree with each other:

# line 116 — predictive entropy divides by M, the generation count
entropy = -torch.sum(mean_across_models, dim=1) / mean_across_models.shape[1]

# line 133 — semantic entropy divides by |C|, the cluster count
entropy = - torch.sum(aggregated_likelihoods, dim=0) / aggregated_likelihoods.shape[0]

Dividing by |C| weights every meaning by 1/|C| — it assumes all meanings equally likely, which is the very thing entropy is supposed to be measuring. With five generations where meaning A is drawn four times and B once:

Implied weights, 4×A and 1×B
WeightingAB
Correct Monte Carlo · ÷ M = 50.800.20
Released code · ÷ |C| = 20.500.50

One off-hand generation counts as much as the answer the model actually believes. Note the two deviations act in opposite directions on the same line — multiplicity over-counts duplicates in the numerator, the |C| divisor under-counts them in the denominator — so fixing them together risks mistaking partial cancellation for one of them being harmless.

Deviation 3 · length normalisation removes the probabilistic reading

The headline call passes -average_neg_log_likelihoods — per-token means, not sums. The logsumexp then computes log Σ p(s|x)^(1/|s|): a sum of per-token geometric-mean probabilities, which is not a probability of anything. The faithful variant sits one line below as unnormalised_entropy_over_concepts and is treated as optional.

Adjacent to it, llh_shift = 5.0 is subtracted with no comment and no counterpart in the paper — rank-preserving, so AUROC-neutral, but it means the reported values are not nats of anything.

What the numbers say

  • ρ = 0.814 is the finding. The estimator choice reorders questions. Every headline number in this literature is AUROC, which sees only ranking — a change that preserved ranking would be invisible and harmless. This one is not.
  • The divisor is the weaker effect. ρ = 0.980 across 18 differing questions: it shifts values without much reordering. Worth knowing before spending time on it.
  • The released values are not entropies. They sit near 5 because llh_shift = 5.0 is subtracted from a log-likelihood already near zero, and their spread tracks likelihood magnitudes rather than how many distinct meanings appeared. The frequency variant lands in [0, log 5] = [0, 1.609] — the genuine attainable range for five samples.
  • Duplicates appear in 13 of 40 questions (20 of 200 generations), and those are exactly the 13 where collapsing them changes the entropy.
08

What follows

The open question is not whether these gaps exist — the table above is a measurement, not an argument — but whether the paper’s claim survives them. Three moves, cheapest first:

  • Cross the three variables. Estimator (released / deduplicated / frequency) × divisor (|C| / M) × normalisation (per-token / summed). Everything needed is already in the pickles, and the opposing signs mean only the crossed design can separate the effects.
  • Report AUROC, not entropy. Every number here describes cluster structure. The paper’s claim is about predicting correctness, and none of this is complete until it runs against exact_match.
  • Bracket the truth on synthetic data. Run the plug-in and nested estimators on a distribution small enough to enumerate exactly. That converts “which of these is biased” from an argument into an interval.

And one interpretive note worth carrying. If the biased estimator scores better on AUROC, the explanation is not that it measures semantic uncertainty more accurately — it is that the likelihoods are smuggling in a length or fluency signal that happens to correlate with correctness. That is a finding too, but a different one.

It also reframes the 2024 Nature follow-up. Moving to a count-based discrete formulation is not a simplification: it abandons a hopeless inner estimation problem over sequence probabilities in favour of an estimand where the frequency estimator is the natural object.
